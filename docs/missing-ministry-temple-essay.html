The Missing Ministry and the Temple

The Missing Ministry and the Temple: Testing the Forty-Day Texts Against Restoration Scripture

Rebuild of Jonah R. Barnes, The Missing Ministry of the Risen Christ (Plain & Precious, 2026), as testable research: canon and Restoration controls set against a staged corpus of post-resurrection and related texts (14 files, 3,019 normalized chunks in data/texts-incoming-normalized/). Companion bibliography of locally held scholarship: docs/missing-ministry-scholarship.md. Working matrices: docs/plans/missing-ministry-matrix-v2.csv, missing-ministry-parallels.md.

Abstract

Acts 1:3 reports that the risen Jesus appeared for forty days, “speaking of the things pertaining to the kingdom of God” — and neither Luke nor any other canonical author records what was said. Barnes, following Hugh Nibley, argues that dozens of early Christian texts do preserve that teaching, and that its content reads like temple instruction: washings, anointings, garments, seals, a shared meal, united prayer, a creation drama, a worthiness charge, and a doctrine of ministry to the dead. This paper reconstructs that argument as a motif-by-motif test, grading each parallel for strength and stating the conditions under which it fails. The finding: the thesis survives first contact. The direct hits — a post-resurrection sacred meal with a unity prayer (Coptic Gospel of the Twelve ↔︎ 3 Nephi 18–19), a baptize→anoint→seal sequence (with vesture conferred in the related investiture discourses), and a directional veil architecture mapping ordinances to temple zones (Gospel of Philip) — are stronger jointly than any one alone. The honest bounds are dating (the best witnesses survive in 3rd–5th-century forms), the lack of a failing control (the Yoma/Tamid baseline remains unrun), and a bibliography Barnes should have engaged but didn’t.

1. The problem: forty days of missing curriculum

The canonical datum is one verse. Acts 1:3 says Jesus “shewed himself alive after his passion by many infallible proofs, being seen of them forty days, and speaking of the things pertaining to the kingdom of God.” Everything else is fragmentary: Luke 24 compresses the appearances into a single day, John 21 adds a lakeside epilogue, 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 preserves what looks like a creedal list of witnesses rather than curriculum. For a church that treats the apostolic commission as its founding charter, the absence of the commission’s content is the New Testament’s most conspicuous silence.

That silence is the whole of Barnes’s opening move, and it is legitimate: something was taught, nothing canonical records it, and the question “what?” is historically well-formed. His answer — that the content survives outside the canon in texts the later church set aside — is where testing must begin, because “set aside” covers everything from second-century orthodoxy (the Epistula Apostolorum) to third-century esoterica (Pistis Sophia) to material even enthusiasts find embarrassing. Not all non-canonical texts are equal witnesses, and a paper that treats them as one pile commits the flattening error critics rightly attack (see §6). The method below exists to prevent exactly that.

2. What the scholarship already says (and where Barnes stands in it)

Three conversations bear on the claim, and Barnes inherits from the first, borrows selectively from the second, and mostly ignores the third. Each needs a sentence of explanation, because the argument of this paper is positioned in the gaps between them.

First, the LDS forty-day lineage. Hugh Nibley’s “Evangelium Quadraginta Dierum” (Vigiliae Christianae 20, 1966; reprinted in CWHN 4) is the founding document: surveying Epistula, the Books of Jeu, the Gospel of the Twelve, the Testament of the Lord, and Pistis Sophia, Nibley argued the forty-day literature forms a coherent corpus organized around four themes — coming apostasy, ordinances given in secrecy to a closed group, descent to the dead with proxy provision, and resurrection instruction — and that 3 Nephi belongs in that corpus. His “Christ Among the Ruins” (1989) pressed the literary point furthest, setting the Coptic Twelve beside 3 Nephi and claiming that with titles removed a scholar would struggle to assign them to different origins. S. Kent Brown and C. Wilfred Griggs popularized the survey for LDS readers (Ensign, 1975; Studies in Scripture 6, 1987), with Brown adding the important caution that Nag Hammadi material is Gnostic and must be used selectively rather than wholesale. John Gee’s Encyclopedia of Mormonism entry (1992) then catalogued the appearances while largely dropping the temple claim, and Scripture Central’s KnoWhy essays (678, 756) restated Nibley’s four themes as Book-of-Mormon evidence — noting, pointedly, that the Coptic Twelve (published 1904) and the Epistula (first Coptic fragment 1895; full Ethiopic text 1912–13) postdate 1830. Barnes is a popularizer in this line: same four-theme skeleton, same 3 Nephi comparator, pitched at a general Latter-day Saint audience. Nothing in this paper disputes the lineage’s existence; the question is whether its parallels survive grading.

Second, LDS temple-origins scholarship. Margaret Barker argued, as a non-LDS Methodist scholar, that Christianity inherits First-Temple theology and ordinance patterns suppressed by the Deuteronomistic reformers — which LDS writers cite as independent corroboration that a restoration would recover temple things (Great High Priest, 2003; Temple Theology, 2004). Kevin Christensen has defended Barker against her critics, notably Eliason, inside LDS journals. Jeffrey Bradshaw fused Barker with Nibley into a full temple-text method, reading Moses and the Enoch literature architecturally (In God’s Image; “Book of Moses as a Temple Text”). Around them stand the FARMS temple volumes (Ricks/Parry/Welch), Parry’s Eden-as-prototype-sanctuary, Welch’s reading of 3 Nephi 11–28 as the Book of Mormon’s Holy of Holies, and Gardner and Skinner as literary-temple readers who stay cautious about direct endowment parallels. On descent specifically, the controls Barnes should cite are Dalton, Bauckham, and Reicke. This literature supplies the paper’s Restoration-side comparators and its vocabulary (veil, vesture, ascent, treasuries); Gardner and Skinner supply its discipline, because they show how far a temple reading can go without claiming an ordinance survival.

Third, non-LDS scholarship on the dialogue gospels — the reckoning. Carl Schmidt’s 1919 edition established the Epistula Apostolorum as a mid-second-century (c. 140–170), anti-Gnostic tract written in the apostles’ names for the universal church — still foundational via e-Clavis (the online bibliography of Christian apocrypha), with later refinements of provenance and stemma (manuscript family-tree). Ron Cameron’s study of the Apocryphon of James placed it as an early-second-century Greek sayings collection inside a Gnosticizing frame: early sayings, late setting, not a liturgy. The critical editions (MacDermot, Meyer, Williams, Kirchner, Hartenstein–Plisch) date 2 Jeu around 250–300 and Pistis Sophia books 1–3 to the late third century, with later strata aware of an emerging canon. Koester read the dialogue gospels as second-century developments of sayings tradition rather than reportage; Perkins showed the post-resurrection dialogue is a literary Gattung — a form that legitimates revelation — rather than minutes of meetings; Marjanen, Thomassen, and Jenkins situated these texts in sectarian, ascetic, cosmological projects, often anti-ecclesial. Bockmuehl’s balanced survey is the control Barnes should have cited. The force of this literature is dating and genre: most of the best temple hits come from third-to-fifth-century Gnostic circles, and their settings legitimate their revelations. Any honest grading must discount for that, and this paper does (§4 calls, §6 objections).

3. Method: controls, corpus, and what counts as a hit

The test needs three fixed points or it proves nothing. Canon seeds establish the baseline idiom: Acts 1:3 (the forty days), Luke 24 and John 20–21 (appearance narratives), 1 Corinthians 15 (witness list), 1 Peter 3:19/4:6 (descent), Hebrews 9 (veil and Holiest), Revelation 2:17/3:5/7:3 (name, raiment, seal), Exodus 28:2 (“holy garments for glory and beauty”), Leviticus 8:6 (washing before vesting), Matthew 27:51/Mark 15:38 (veil rent). Restoration controls supply the comparator: 3 Nephi 11 (multitude round about the temple, a descending Man in white, Hosanna, worship), 3 Nephi 17–20 (sacrament prayers, thrice-amen united prayer, unity petitions), Moroni 4–5, D&C 76/88/110/131–132/138, D&C 124:39 (anointings, washings, baptisms for the dead, solemn assemblies in one breath), Moses 1 and Abraham 3 (council, creation, fall).

The witness corpus was staged public-domain-only: Pistis Sophia complete (Mead 1921 — Mead numbers chapters continuously across his four Books, so ch. 163 falls in the Second Book; Schmidt/MacDermot number differently, hence Book+chapter given throughout); 1–2 Jeu in Schmidt’s 1905 German (no public-domain English exists — MacDermot and Robinson remain copyrighted, so this paper reads Jeu in German with glosses); Epistula Apostolorum complete in 51 chapters (James 1924); Questions of Bartholomew (James 1924); Apocalypse of Paul in the Latin Visio (ANF 9 — the Greek recension in James 1924 stands as an unchecked supplement); the Coptic Gospel of the Twelve in Revillout’s PO II summary with French excerpt (distinguished throughout from the Syriac Harris text that shares its name — conflating them is the standard error and this paper does not make it); Apocryphon of James as outline plus links (no public-domain full text exists); Thomas as a saying index and Philip complete locally (Isenberg, 876 lines verified); 1 Clement complete, all seven Ignatian letters, Justin (both Apologies complete, Dialogue excerpts, Resurrection fragments), Irenaeus excerpts. Second-Temple controls come from the database: 1–2 Enoch, Jubilees, Testament of Levi 8, Testament of Abraham, 11Q13 Melchizedek, 4Q521, the Sabbath Songs, the Rule and Hodayot.

Grading runs motif by motif across fourteen rows (chart, §5). A + marks a direct parallel with a citable reference; ~ a thematic echo; - absence; each with high/medium confidence. The load-bearing methodological claim is the sequence test: isolated furniture (a robe here, a meal there) can be coincidence or generic temple culture, but ordered multi-step sequences — baptize, then anoint, then seal, then clothe, then feast — are harder to borrow by accident and harder to genericize away. Conversely the test’s known weakness, stated here rather than discovered later: no failing control has been run. Until Sinai procedure (Yoma/Tamid) is scored through the same fourteen rows to show what a non-endowment temple text looks like, every + carries a parallelomania discount (Sandmel 1962). The grades below already include it.

4. The evidence, motif by motif

The sequence is the argument. Pistis Sophia’s late books move souls through three ordered steps: “those of the Midst will baptize them and give unto them the spiritual unction and seal them” (Mead Bk 2, ch. 163 [chunk 1231]). The order matters more than the vocabulary: washing first as preparation, anointing as consecration, sealing as authorization — by analogy with the Leviticus 8 pattern (wash, then vest Aaron), extended here into a fuller initiatory arc. Jeu 2 independently gives three baptisms — water, fire, and Holy Ghost — followed by “the mystery of the spiritual anointing” (Mysterium der geistigen Salbung), then seals and archon-names over the treasuries, then vesture. Two witnesses, same skeleton, neither copying the other’s words. D&C 124:39 gathers the identical furniture set — “anointings, and your washings, and your baptisms for the dead, and your solemn assemblies” — though in a different list order (anointings first), so the shared point is the furniture plus the Pistis/Jeu-internal sequence, not an identical ordering with modern revelation. That distinction is exactly the kind this method must keep making. Grade: med-high.

Vesture given with authority, not merely worn. On the Mount of Olives, Pistis Sophia’s Jesus announces “the times are completed for me to put on my Vesture” and then, vested, “all authority hath been given me” (Mead Bk 1, ch. 11; chunks 275, 277). The garment here is an investiture event: putting on confers authority, and the text stages the two as one motion. The Twelve’s ninth fragment matches the grammar from the recipient side: “he clothed us with power for our apostolate” — clothing as commissioning. Against these set 3 Nephi 11:8 (the descending Man “clothed in a white robe”), D&C 138:14 (the righteous dead “clothed with purity”), and Revelation 3:5 (white raiment to overcomers). The link is the verb cluster — put on, clothe, with power, with authority — not whiteness alone, which Revelation shares with half of antiquity. Grade: high. (Mead references are to G.R.S. Mead, Pistis Sophia, 2nd rev. ed. (Watkins, 1921), the public-domain translation staged in data/texts-incoming/; chunk numbers are normalizer ordinals in data/texts-incoming-normalized/, not Mead’s pages.)

Veil architecture with directions. The Gospel of Philip maps three Jerusalem buildings by orientation — west-facing “Holy” equals baptism, south-facing “Holy of the Holy” equals redemption, east-facing “Holy of Holies” equals the bridal chamber — then rends the veil “from top to bottom. For it was fitting for some from below to go upward” (local lines 490–500; orientations per Isenberg’s translation). This is ascent given as floor plan: zones, gates, and a torn barrier with a stated purpose (ascent from below). Philip also pairs the elements theologically — “fitting to baptize in the two, in the light and the water. Now the light is the chrism” — so washing and anointing are one action in two media. Hebrews 9:3 places the Holiest “after the second veil,” the Synoptics rend it at the death (Matthew 27:51), and modern revelation keeps the rent-veil idiom for unveiling (D&C 38:8, 67:10, 101:23). Barnes’s ascent row is really a veil row, and Philip is its best witness. Grade: high.

The Jeu altar scene nobody quotes. Jeu 2 stages what reads as a worked rite rather than a described one: an incense altar (Weihrauchaltar) improvised on underbrush, layered with grapevines, juniper berries, betel, asbestos, agate, and frankincense; disciples robed in linen garments (leinene Kleider), wreathed, frankincense placed in their mouths, with the number of the first amen, 530, laid in their hand; then a cup of wine and breads apportioned to the number of the disciples, olive branches set on the place. Every element has a Sinai precedent (linen in Exodus 28, altar aromatics, numbered portions) — but the recombination as a single Christian mystery working has no Sinai precedent at all. The right question is the paper’s standing one: scored blind, does this scene sit closer to Sinai procedure or to a latter-day endowment session? Linen, wreath, mouth, number, cup, numbered breads, olive: the density is suggestive, not probative — suggestive because no Sinai rite combines these elements, not probative because the Yoma/Tamid control that would certify the comparison is unrun. Grade: med-high for the recombination (the exotic aromatics read as late-Egyptian overlay, discounted accordingly).

Meal as ordered sacrament. The Twelve’s second fragment gives Christ “eating with them at the table of the earth, reminding them of the table of his kingdom” — the earthly meal explicitly typological of a heavenly one — and praying the Father “that they may be one, as we are one,” glossed to John 17:11 at PO II p.134. The sixth fragment orders the fellowship: a turning table so every hand reaches what the Lord eats and blesses, all taking salt together. Set 3 Nephi 18 and 20 (the sacramental prayers, given verbatim), Moroni 4–5, and the unity petitions (John 17:21; 3 Nephi 19:23, 29) alongside, and the parallel is structural: post-resurrection setting, shared blessed meal, oneness prayer, commissioning force. This is Barnes’s lead parallel and it earns the lead. Grade: high.

Small liturgical fingerprints: amen thrice, and the oath before mysteries. 3 Nephi 19’s united prayer is answered “Amen” three times; Jeu 1’s disciples answer “Amen, Amen, Amen — dreimal.” Responsa patterns are graded evidence rather than proof: triple answers occur naturally in biblical idiom too (Numbers 6:24–26, Isaiah 6:3), and Jeu 1’s amens answer angelic names rather than united prayer — but the 3 Nephi 19 correspondence is close enough to keep on the map. Likewise Jeu 2 bars recipients from false swearing, swearing at all, and fornication before the mysteries are imparted, and withholds the prayer texts from outsiders “for the goods of this world” — a worthiness gate fused to a secrecy charge, matching 3 Nephi 26:14 (withheld utterance), Mormon 8:14 (sealed record), and D&C 84:19–22 (oath-bound covenant). This is Nibley’s closed-group theme in its purest form. Grades: ~ echo at med (amen — different liturgical object plus biblical idiom makes coincidence the live alternative); med-high (oath — as sociology of secrecy; generic mystery-cult esotericism explains the form, the worthiness-fusion is the distinctive part).

Descent with names. Bartholomew’s devil demands of Hades its captive (“this prophet will we take”), Paul tours punishment and throne (Visio §§42, 44 — worm, gnashing, then altar, veil, throne, twenty-four elders), the Twelve pairs Judas with Adam “descending to the Tartarus of Amenti,” and the Epistula commissions the risen one’s witnesses — against 1 Peter 3:19/4:6 and the full D&C 138 program of organized ministry and proxy ordinance. A scruple belongs here: Paul’s tour is judgment literature, and this paper stages it as a control (punishment frame) rather than a witness; the Hades-dialogue texts are the ones that actually parallel the descent kerygma — kerygma meaning proclamation, here preaching to the dead. Grade: med-high for the dialogues, control-only for Paul.

Bridal-chamber concentration, honestly sourced. Marriage idiom clusters in Philip (the five mysteries culminating in the bridal chamber; the chamber reserved; the temple-ascent mapping) and Thomas (saying 75: solitaries enter the bridal chamber; 104: the bridegroom’s presence suspends fasting; 37: garment-stripping without shame), supported in the Epistula by the wise-and-foolish-virgins bridal discourse (chs. 43–45; the Cana wedding setting of ch. 5 is a thinner echo). It does not occur in Jeu (zero Braut hits in either book) and barely in Pistis — so the marriage material is sourced to Philip/Thomas and not inflated elsewhere. Grade: high for Philip as mystagogy (bridal-chamber sacramentalism with temple-ascent mapping); the bridge to D&C 131–132 marriage ordinance does not follow, since Valentinian chambers are unconsummated mysticism and Thomas’s stripping is encratite asceticism — both cut against, not for, the latter-day comparator. Echo elsewhere.

6. Objections, stated fairly and answered where possible

Late dating. Only the Epistula plausibly reaches the mid-second century; the strongest temple hits survive in third-to-fifth-century Gnostic forms. The paper’s answer is partial but real: certainty is withheld and five rows reach high rather than proven; the Epistula carries the early anchor, and the sequence test does not depend on first-century reportage — ordered initiatory grammar can transmit through redaction the way liturgy generally does. What would strengthen this reply is hostile-witness material (Ginza, Mani Psalm-Book, Celsus), still unbuilt.

Parallelomania and generic furniture (Sandmel 1962). White robes, sacred meals, and guarded secrets are common ancient-Christian topoi — recurring themes — (Revelation 7, 1 Corinthians 15, 2 Corinthians 12, the disciplina arcani, the early church’s discipline of guarding sacramental rites from outsiders) — stacking generic hits proves nothing. The paper’s direct reply is the sequence test of §3: furniture is generic, ordered multi-step grammar is not, and the Yoma/Tamid failing control is named as the experiment that would close the question. Until it runs, every + above already carries the discount.

Adversarial strata. Schmidt’s foundational work shows the Epistula is anti-Gnostic polemic while Pistis and Jeu are Gnostic ascent magic; Nibley flattens enemies into one “forgotten heritage.” Conceded in part: Paul is staged here as control, the Epistula’s polemical frame is noted in its row, and no claim in §4 requires Pistis and Epistula to agree theologically — only to preserve overlapping initiatory grammar, which adversaries can share with a common ancestor.

Barker’s method and the 3 Nephi comparator. Mainstream critics find Barker’s Josianic-suppression thesis under-evidenced, and Christensen’s defense is LDS-internal; likewise no non-LDS scholar admits 3 Nephi as a comparator, and “unavailable in 1830” assumes what criticism denies (KJV idiom, primitivist restorationism, and Masonic-adjacent temple language suffice for generic hits). These are boundary conditions, not refutations: the paper bounds them rather than settling them, and its positive case does not depend on Barker or on persuading anyone about 3 Nephi — the pre-1830 publication facts (Coptic Twelve 1904; Epistula fragment 1895, full text 1912–13) do that work independently.

7. Conclusion

Barnes’s method reproduces: Restoration text as control, forty-day texts as witnesses, Nibley’s four themes as the skeleton to test against. What the test returns is not a blanket vindication but a graded map — direct hits on meal, unity, commissioning power, and veil architecture; suggestive sequences on washing, anointing, sealing, and vesture; honest zeroes (marriage in Jeu, temple content in Paul-as-witness) where the corpus refuses to cooperate. The pattern across rows is what carries the thesis: independent witnesses converging on the same initiatory grammar, in settings each text presents as the risen Christ teaching his apostles. The work remaining is concrete — run the Yoma/Tamid control, build the hostile-witness rows, commission a public-domain Apocryphon of James — and each item either strengthens the grades above or revises them. That revisability is the point: a claim about ancient roots should be testable, and now it is.
