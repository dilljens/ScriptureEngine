# Ascending to Presence: Isaiah, the Psalms, and the Temple Path to Sanctification

*An article on how Isaiah teaches a people to become holy enough to abide the presence of God, read through the keys in Nephi, the words of Christ, the Doctrine and Covenants, and the Book of Mormon.*

## Introduction: Isaiah saw what Nephi saw

Nephi introduces his long Isaiah quotations with a claim that is easy to pass over:

> He verily saw my Redeemer, even as I have seen him. And my brother, Jacob, also has seen him as I have seen him. (:verse[2ne.11.2-3])

For Nephi, Isaiah is not primarily a political prophet or a poet of judgment. He is a witness who has stood where Nephi has stood, writing so that others may follow. Hence the invitation that closes the introduction: readers may liken these words to themselves and be lifted up to rejoice (:verse[2ne.11.8]).

Jesus gives the same text a striking endorsement. To the Nephites he gives a commandment to search the words of Isaiah diligently, for they are great, concern both Israel and the Gentiles, and describe things that have been and shall be (:verse[3ne.23.1-3]). The Doctrine and Covenants adds its own exposition of Isaiah 11 and 52, reading them as a description of latter-day gathering, priesthood authority, and a return that leads to revelation (D&C 113).

The question that guides this article is the one Isaiah and the temple liturgy share: what changes in a person and a people so that they can endure and enjoy the presence of God rather than flee from it?

Scripture answers consistently. Without holiness no one shall see the Lord (:verse[heb.12.14]). Those who ascend the hill of the Lord are those with innocent hands and a pure heart (:verse[psa.24.3-4]). Every soul who forsakes sin, comes unto Christ, calls on his name, obeys his voice, and keeps his commandments shall see his face (:verse[dc93.93.1]). Sanctified minds become single to God, and in time the veil is unveiled (:verse[dc88.88.68]).

## How to read Isaiah for sanctification

Nephi gives four practical keys in :verse[2ne.25.1-8]:

1. The difficulty is partly cultural. Isaiah uses the manner of prophesying among the Jews — parallelism, covenant lawsuit, symbolic geography.
2. Rabbinic background alone does not unlock him. Nephi deliberately did not teach that manner at length.
3. The decisive key is the spirit of prophecy, which is the testimony of Jesus. Those filled with it find Isaiah plain.
4. The lens is latter-day fulfillment. Isaiah writes of scattering and gathering, suffering and glory, and those who live in the day of fulfillment understand more readily.

The method that follows from these keys is simple: read for Christ, read for covenant Israel and Gentiles together, read for past type and latter-day antitype together, and liken each invitation to wash, return, and ascend.

## What Hebrew clarifies

A few Hebrew terms, used carefully, prevent common misunderstandings.

Holiness (*qadosh*) means set apart for divine use, not merely moral goodness. The verb to make holy is *qiddesh* (Piel), and to sanctify oneself is *hitqaddesh* (Hitpael, as in :verse[josh.3.5]). The sanctuary (*miqdash*) is the place where what is set apart dwells. The threefold Holy in :verse[isa.6.3] is a superlative: wholly other.

Clean and holy are not identical. *Tahor* means ritually fit to approach. *Tame* means unfit. *Chol* means common. :verse[psa.24.3-4] uses still different words — *neqi khappayim* (innocent of hands) and *bar-levav* (pure of heart) — innocence and clarity rather than cultic fitness. :verse[isa.1.16] uses two verbs: *rachatsu* (wash, Qal imperative) and *hizzakku* (make yourselves clean, Hitpael). Only the second is reflexive in form; the point stands that human participation is required. :verse[isa.52.11] uses *hibbaru* (Niphal of *barar*, be purified or selected), not the same verb.

Glory (*kavod*) carries the sense of weight. Presence has mass that must be borne. When the Doctrine and Covenants says Israel could not endure his presence (:verse[dc84.84.23]) or that sanctified beings can abide celestial glory (:verse[dc76.76.116-118]), it is describing capacity, not only permission.

Face (*panim*) means presence as well as countenance. To seek the face is to seek audience. The hiding of the face (:verse[isa.8.17]; :verse[isa.59.2]) is exile. Face to face is return. To know (*yada* in :verse[isa.52.6]) is covenant knowledge by experience.

Return (*shuv*) is the characteristic word for repentance: turn and come home (:verse[isa.55.7]; :verse[isa.30.15]). Atonement (*kaphar*) means to cover. :verse[isa.6.7] reads *tekhuppar* (Pual, shall be atoned for): sin is covered by what comes from the altar.

Hearing (*shema*) implies obeying. :verse[isa.50.4] describes an ear wakened morning by morning. Waiting (*qavah* in :verse[isa.40.31]; :verse[isa.30.18]) means to hope and expect; the image of braiding or binding is homiletical rather than firm etymology and should be used as such.

Crushed (*dakka*), lowly (*shefal-ruach*), poor (*ani*), smitten (*nekheh*), and trembling (*chared*) describe the柔 posture :verse[isa.57.15] and :verse[isa.66.2] associate with indwelling: the high and holy One dwells with the contrite, and looks to the one who trembles at his word. Trembling here is reverent seriousness, not panic.

Calling is reciprocal in Isaiah's rhetoric. Isaiah answers *hineni, shelacheni* (here am I, send me) in :verse[isa.6.8]. The Lord answers those who call with *hineni* (here I am) in :verse[isa.58.9]. The exchange should be described as theological reciprocity, not as a grammatical formula in every psalm; :verse[psa.134.1], for example, uses *hinneh* (behold), not *hineni*.

Literary patterns function as teaching. Parallelism deepens by restatement (:verse[isa.1.18] scarlet and crimson made snow and wool). The covenant lawsuit (*rib* in Isaiah 1 and 41-44) establishes honesty. Woe oracles (*hoy* in Isaiah 5, six times) pronounce judgment; Isaiah's own *oy-li* (woe is me) in :verse[isa.6.5] owns it personally. Repetition such as comfort ye or awake awake signals completeness and urgency. Proposed chiasms and inclusios should be labeled as proposed unless the structure is demonstrated.

## The Psalms, temple worship, and order

LeGrand Baker and Stephen Ricks, in *Who Shall Ascend into the Hill of the Lord?* (2009; 2nd ed. 2011), propose that the Psalms preserve the libretto of First-Temple worship associated with the New Year and the Feast of Tabernacles: creation remembered, the fall acknowledged, atonement made, the veil approached, kingship renewed, instruction given, and a feast shared. On this reading, Psalm 24 is an entrance liturgy — creation confessed (verses 1-2), entry requirements asked and answered (verses 3-6), gates commanded to lift for the King of glory (verses 7-10) — and Psalm 23 summarizes the same movement from pasture to valley to house forever.

Baker and Ricks further argue that the present order of the Psalter is post-exilic and liturgical rather than chronological or dramatic, and they attempt to reconstruct an earlier dramatic sequence. That reconstruction remains their proposal and depends on form-critical and comparative judgments associated with Gunkel, Mowinckel, Barker, and Nibley. It is best used as a lens for comparison with Isaiah rather than as settled fact. Their work is still under copyright; the authorized highlights sampler may be cited, but the full text should be consulted in print.

A separate work is sometimes confused with theirs because of the similar title: L. Michael Morales, *Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the Lord?* (2015), a biblical theology of Leviticus that takes :verse[psa.24.3] as its epigraph but does not discuss Psalter arrangement. Morales traces exile to tabernacle to Day of Atonement to Zion and to Christ.

Within the Psalter itself, Psalms 120-134, each entitled a Song of Ascents (*shir hamaalot*), form a pilgrimage collection from distress at a distance to blessing in the house by night. The movement can be summarized without forcing every psalm into a rigid station: alienation named (120), help lifted toward the Keeper (121), glad arrival at the house (122), lowly supplication (123), deliverance remembered (124), stability as Zion (125), joy after tears (126), house built by the Lord rather than anxiety (127), table blessed (128), affliction endured without prevailing (129), depths confessed and forgiveness found (130), soul quieted as a weaned child (131), resting place found for the ark (132), unity as anointing oil and dew (133), servants blessing by night and blessed from Zion (134). Key Hebrew markers include ascent (*alah*), keeping (*shamar*), beholding (*hineh*), and the arc from peace sought to blessing given (*shalom* to *berakah*).

## Isaiah as an ascent map

Read in canonical order, Isaiah repeats one ascent at increasing depth: conviction, washing, purging by fire, reverent hearing, covenant clothing, and indwelling.

Isaiah 1-12 establishes the foundation. Isaiah 1 invites reasoning and washing that turns scarlet to snow. Isaiah 2 invites ascent to the house to learn his ways. Isaiah 4 describes filth washed and blood purged by a spirit of burning, after which those who remain are called holy and covered as by a tabernacle. Isaiah 6 gives the paradigm: seeing the enthroned King, confessing uncleanness, being touched from the altar, hearing the voice, and answering with consecration. Isaiah 7-10 tests whether Israel will ask, believe, sanctify the Lord himself, and stay upon the Holy One rather than human alliance. Isaiah 11-12 ends in a holy mountain without hurt and a song because the Holy One is in the midst.

Isaiah 13-27 presents judgment as cleansing. Each oracle names an allegiance incompatible with presence — imperial pride, self-sufficiency, forgotten Maker, worldly wisdom, revelry without reverence, commodified wealth — and shows it brought low so that a remnant learns trust. The sequence culminates in a mountain feast where the veil is destroyed, death is swallowed, tears are wiped, gates open to the righteous, and a trumpet gathers to the holy mount (Isaiah 24-27).

Isaiah 28-39 trains discernment of voices. False trusts recur: lies as refuge, lip service, Egyptian horses, political flattery. Sanctification reverses the flow toward returning and rest, quietness and confidence, and ears that hear direction behind them (:verse[isa.30.15-21]). :verse[isa.33.14-17] states the entrance question directly: who shall dwell with devouring fire? The answer — righteous walk and speech, just dealing, refusal of bribes and violence to ear and eye — parallels Psalms 15 and 24 and temple worthiness. Isaiah 35 then describes the protected highway called the Way of Holiness.

Isaiah 40-48 offers comfort after exile and teaches the difference between gods that must be carried and a God who carries from womb to gray hairs (:verse[isa.46.3-4]). Waiting renews strength (40:31). Transgression is blotted for his own sake (43:25). Spirit is poured on the thirsty (44:3). All ends are invited to look and be saved (45:22). The furnace of affliction refines, and the command comes to go forth from Babylon with singing (48:10, 20).

Isaiah 49-57 stands at the center. The Servant listens morning by morning, gives his back to smiters, sets his face like flint, is wounded for transgression, and by knowledge justifies many (:verse[isa.50.4-7]; :verse[isa.53.5], :verse[isa.53.11]). Because he bears iniquity and intercedes, he can say to prisoners to go forth. Zion, asleep in dust, is told three times to awake, put on strength and beautiful garments, shake dust, loose neck bands, depart from uncleanness, and be clean (Isaiah 51-52). The promise is that watchmen shall see eye to eye (52:8). The barren are told to sing and enlarge the tent (54:1-2). All who thirst are invited to come without price, incline the ear, seek while near, and forsake wayward thoughts for abundant pardon (55:1-7). Sabbath keepers who take hold of the covenant, including the stranger and eunuch, receive a place and an everlasting name and joy in the house of prayer for all people (56:4-7). The high and holy One dwells with the contrite to revive them (57:15).

Isaiah 58-66 moves from corrected worship to indwelling. The acceptable fast looses wickedness and lifts oppression, after which light breaks and the Lord answers (58:6-9). Sabbath kept as delight leads to riding on high places (58:13-14). Iniquity that separates is confessed, and the Redeemer comes to those who turn (59:1-2, 20-21). Zion is told to arise and shine, is clothed with salvation as with a robe, receives a new name, and posts watchmen who give God no rest until Jerusalem is praise (60-62). Intercession recalls that the angel of his presence saved and bore Israel (63:9), then pleads for the heavens to be rent and confesses that humanity is clay in the Father's hands (64:1, 8). The Lord answers that he was already seeking, promises new heavens and a new earth, looks to the poor and contrite who tremble at his word, and gathers all nations to see his glory (65-66).

## Temple symbols across the ascent

Several symbols recur and should be understood together rather than in isolation.

The gate poses the entrance question. Psalms 15 and 24, :verse[isa.33.14-17], and Isaiah 56 describe moral qualifications: truthful walk and speech, just dealing, clean hands, Sabbath fidelity, covenant hold. The gate does not celebrate lineage; it examines formation.

Washing and purging prepare capacity. :verse[isa.1.16], :verse[isa.4.4], and :verse[isa.6.7] associate water, burning, and altar coal with removal of filth, blood, and iniquity of lips. In Latter-day Saint terms this corresponds to baptism, reception of the Holy Ghost, and the sanctification described in :verse[alma.13.11-13] and :verse[3ne.19.28-29], where disciples are purified in Christ.

Garments mark the change. Putting on beautiful garments (:verse[isa.52.1]; :verse[isa.61.10]), white robes through sanctification (:verse[alma.13.11-13]; :verse[rev.7.14]), and shaking dust (:verse[isa.52.2]; :verse[moro.10.30-31]) belong together. Moroni's invitation to awake, shake dust, deny ungodliness, love God, and be perfected in Christ through grace unto spotlessness (:verse[moro.10.30-33]) directly echoes Isaiah 52.

The veil marks the threshold of knowledge. :verse[isa.25.7] describes a covering over all nations to be destroyed on the mountain. Ether describes a veil of unbelief to be rent through prayer in the name of Christ (:verse[ether.4.13-15]). The brother of Jared, through knowledge and exceeding faith, could not be kept from beholding within the veil and then saw the Lord (:verse[ether.3.19-20]). Sanctification through grace precedes enduring the glory (:verse[dc76.76.116-118]).

The feast anticipates communion. :verse[isa.25.6-9] joins abundance, unveiled face, swallowed death, and wiped tears in one mountain meal. :verse[isa.55.1-2] invites without price. The sacrament, taken in remembrance with the promise of his Spirit, rehearses the same logic on a weekly scale (:verse[3ne.18.7], :verse[3ne.18.11]).

The ark's rest describes arrival. Psalm 132 seeks a resting place (*menuchah*) for the ark until the Lord chooses Zion as his habitation forever. :verse[isa.11.10] calls the Messiah's rest glorious. Entering into his rest (:verse[alma.12.34-37]; Hebrews 4) means entering his presence, not merely ceasing labor.

Anointing and unity describe overflow. Psalm 133 compares unity to oil on Aaron and dew on Hermon, with blessing commanded as life forever. :verse[isa.61.1] describes anointing to heal and liberate. :verse[isa.32.15] and :verse[isa.44.3] describe Spirit poured from on high and upon descendants. Unity makes priesthood fellowship visible.

Night service describes abiding. Psalm 134 depicts servants standing by night, lifting hands, blessing and being blessed from Zion. :verse[isa.62.6-7] depicts watchmen who do not keep silence day or night. :verse[isa.30.29] depicts festival song in the night on the way to the mountain. Abiding continues when sight fails.

## The temple ascent as return to Eden

Isaiah presents sanctification as ascent back to Edenic presence. After transgression, the Lord placed cherubim and a flaming sword to guard the way of the tree of life (:verse[gen.3.24]). The way was barred, not erased. Isaiah's promise is that the barren place itself becomes the garden again, for the Lord will comfort Zion and make her wilderness like Eden (:verse[isa.51.3]).

The means of return is a prepared way. Isaiah describes a highway in the wilderness where the ransomed of the Lord shall return (:verse[isa.35.8], 10), and a voice commanding that in the desert a highway be prepared, with the result that the glory of the Lord shall be revealed (:verse[isa.40.3-5]). The end of that highway resembles its beginning. John beheld a pure river of water of life proceeding from the throne, with the tree of life on either side, and the promise that his servants shall see his face (:verse[rev.22.1-5]). Exile from the garden closes with dwelling before the face.

Within that movement, washing marks re-entry and garments mark restored covering. Defilement is not carried inward. Purity is both commanded and given, expressed in the distinction between *qiddesh*, to sanctify or consecrate by divine action, and *hitqaddesh*, to present oneself as sanctified. Rest, *menuchah*, then denotes more than cessation. It is Sabbath dwelling, residence in the place prepared for the Presence.

### Stations of the ascent

The ascent proceeds through ordered stations, each with moral preparation corresponding to sacred space.

At the outer gate stands the question of worthiness. Who shall ascend into the hill of the Lord? The answer requires *neqi* hands and *bar* heart, clean hands and a pure heart (:verse[psa.24.3-4]; :verse[psa.15.1-2]). Isaiah renders the same inquiry as prophetic trembling: who among us shall dwell with the devouring fire? — followed by the walk of righteousness, truthful speech, and refusal of bribery and bloodshed, whose result is dwelling on high and beholding the king in his beauty (:verse[isa.33.14-17]). The altar of sacrifice stands at this threshold, where what is offered is relinquished and what remains is examined.

Then comes washing at the laver. Isaiah's call is direct: wash you, make you clean (:verse[isa.1.16]). The promise answers that the Lord will wash the filth of the daughters of Zion by the spirit of judgment and by the spirit of burning (:verse[isa.4.4]). The related command, *hibbaru*, be pure, is addressed to the bearers of the vessels of the Lord as they depart from defilement (:verse[isa.52.11]). Purity precedes approach.

Illumination follows purification. Isaiah summons Zion to walk in the light of the Lord (:verse[isa.2.5]) and later announces, arise, shine, for thy light is come (:verse[isa.60.1]). The psalm of arrival records gladness at coming to the house of the Lord (:verse[psa.122.1]), where light is received and shared. Joy replaces distance.

Nearer the veil, the senses are humbled. Isaiah's vision in the temple leaves the prophet undone: woe is me, for I am undone, because I am a man of unclean lips (:verse[isa.6.5]). A seraph touches the mouth with a coal from the altar, declaring *tekhuppar*, that iniquity is atoned and sin purged (:verse[isa.6.6-7]). The psalms of depth and childhood belong here: the cry out of the depths (:verse[psa.130.1]) and the quieted soul as a child that is weaned (:verse[psa.131.2]). Speech is cleansed and self-assertion is stilled.

Within, indwelling is described as ark rest. The psalm of habitation seeks a place for the Lord until he declares, this is my rest for ever, here will I dwell (:verse[psa.132.5], 14). Isaiah likewise foresees the root of Jesse standing as an ensign, with his rest glorious (:verse[isa.11.10]). Yet heaven itself cannot contain that rest, for heaven is his throne and earth his footstool, and he looks to him that is poor and of a contrite spirit (:verse[isa.66.1-2]). The holy of holies is thus both place and person made fit for Presence.

Anointing and unity follow indwelling. The oil descending upon the head and beard, bringing blessing and life for evermore (:verse[psa.133.2-3]), corresponds to Isaiah's anointed commission: the Spirit of the Lord God upon me, to bring good tidings, bind the brokenhearted, appoint beauty for ashes, and clothe mourning with the garment of praise (:verse[isa.61.1-3]). Consecration overflows into restoration of others.

Finally, ministry extends outward by night. Those who stand by night in the house of the Lord lift hands in holiness and bless from Zion (:verse[psa.134.1-3]). Isaiah's watchmen carry the same office, appointed to never hold their peace day nor night and to give the Lord no rest till he make Jerusalem a praise in the earth (:verse[isa.62.6-7]). Blessing received in Presence is carried back toward the world.

### The pilgrim order

The Songs of Ascents, Psalms 120-134, preserve the pilgrim order for these stations in sequence: distress among alien tongues (120), lifted eyes toward the Keeper (121), glad arrival at Jerusalem (122), mercy sought under contempt (123), remembered help (124), stability of trust (125), return from captivity as dreamers bearing sheaves (126), the Lord as builder of house and family (127), blessing from Zion upon obedient labor (128), affliction endured without prevailing evil (129), waiting from the depths (130), childlike stillness (131), habitation and *menuchah* rest with the ark (132), anointing oil and dew-like unity (133), and night blessing flowing outward from the house (134).

## Book of Mormon witnesses

Three Book of Mormon readings are particularly important for Isaiah 49-53.

Abinadi reads Isaiah 53 as the suffering of God himself, who subjects flesh to Spirit, breaks the bands of death, and thereby gains power to intercede and resurrect (Mosiah 14; :verse[mosiah.15.1-13]; :verse[mosiah.16.6-9]). His seed are those who believe the report. Beautiful feet that publish salvation (:verse[isa.52.7-10]; :verse[mosiah.15.14-18]) proclaim the price that stripes paid.

Jacob reads Isaiah 49-51 as covenant history centered on Christ. Scattering is real, divorce imagery is severe, and awakening is commanded — look to the rock, awake, put on strength, loose neck bands — but the infinite atonement satisfies justice and gathers those who reconcile themselves to God (2 Nephi 6-10, especially :verse[2ne.9.8-26]).

Nephi reads Isaiah 48-49 as latter-day gathering. Those who swear without truth are refined in affliction and commanded to go forth from Babylon with singing. The Servant becomes a light to the Gentiles, prisoners go forth, Zion is graven on divine palms, nursing kings and queens bear children home, and all flesh comes to know the Redeemer (1 Nephi 20-22).

Together they show that Isaiah's Servant and Zion passages are not abstract poetry. They describe a Redeemer who suffers to loose bands and a people who awake, cleanse, and gather to his house.

## Doctrine and Covenants and other witnesses

The Doctrine and Covenants gathers the same themes into house theology. Priesthood ordinances manifest godliness, and without them no one can see the face of God; Israel's hardening left them unable to endure presence (:verse[dc84.84.19-24]). Sanctified, single minds have eyes opened and the veil rent (:verse[dc88.88.68]). The fivefold path of forsaking, coming, calling, obeying, and keeping leads to seeing his face (:verse[dc93.93.1]). If his people keep his sayings in his house, they may behold his face; his glory rests where no unclean thing is permitted (:verse[dc97.97.15-17]). The command to go out from Babylon without haste or flight (:verse[dc38.38.42]; :verse[dc133.133.5-7]) and to set watchmen and build a tower (:verse[dc101.101.43-62]) preserves what the house creates. The Kirtland temple prayer asks that his name be upon the house, his glory rest upon his people, angels have charge, and all who enter feel his power (D&C 109).

Textual honesty belongs to the same ascent. A telling case is the Lectures on Faith's fifth lecture (1835, principally Sidney Rigdon's drafting under Joseph's editorial approval), which teaches two personages with the Father a "personage of spirit" — against the later formulation that the Father and Son are embodied and the Holy Ghost a spirit personage (:verse[dc130.130.22-23]). Historians across perspectives read this as development rather than scandal: doctrine articulated line upon line from the early 1830s through Nauvoo, with embodied Father and Son multiply attested in 1841-44 sermons and the King Follett Discourse. They also note the Ramus record itself is mediated — Clayton's habitually expanded notes, Richards's reconstruction without presence, first printing thirteen years later, and an 1850s copyist's gloss supplying the final explanatory clause — so the doctrine stands as Joseph's 1843 correction while the sentence explaining why is later editorial theology, knowingly retained. The rule for readers: cite the doctrine as his, hold the sentence loosely, and never flatten the arc with either text.

The Book of Mormon adds persons who embody the path. The brother of Jared brings molten stones in darkness, asks plainly for light, sees within the veil through knowledge, and then sees the Lord through exceeding faith (Ether 3-4). Moroni condenses Isaiah 52 into awake, shake, deny, love, come, and be perfected unto spotlessness (:verse[moro.10.30-33]). Alma describes sanctified garments made white, inability to look on sin except with abhorrence, and entrance into rest (:verse[alma.13.11-13]), and asks whether the image of Christ is in the countenance (:verse[alma.5.14], 19). The Nephite disciples are purified and sanctified in Christ as he is in the Father (:verse[3ne.19.28-29]). Ordinances form the gate: baptism and fire with enduring to the end (:verse[2ne.31.13-17]), covenant bearing of burdens (:verse[mosiah.18.8-10]), and rebirth through water, blood, and Spirit (:verse[moses.6.60-61]).

Hezekiah's narrative in Isaiah 36-39 illustrates the same movement in story form rather than oracle: silence before the accuser at the gate, spreading the threatening letter before the Lord in the house, turning the face to the wall in weeping prayer, receiving added life with sins cast behind the back, vowing song in the house, and then the warning that unguarded display of treasures to Babylon invites captivity. It is usefully read alongside Enos's wrestling, Nephi's psalm, and Liberty-era prayer as an example of bringing accusation and sickness to the house rather than answering them at the wall. The distinction between *hinneh*, behold as disclosure, and *hineni*, here am I as availability, governs the whole: Presence is revealed, and the sanctified pilgrim answers with consecrated readiness.

## The straight and narrow path

Nephi gives the clearest exposition of the straight and narrow path in scripture. In 2 Nephi 31, the doctrine of Christ unfolds in sequence: faith in Christ, repentance, baptism by water, baptism by fire and of the Holy Ghost, and enduring to the end (:verse[2ne.31.10-14]). Baptism is described as the gate: the gate by which ye should enter is repentance and baptism by water (:verse[2ne.31.17]). Beyond the gate lies a course marked by its straitness and narrowness, walked by reliance on Christ rather than by human wisdom. The continuation describes life on the path: feasting upon the words of Christ, praying with diligence and without ceasing, and pressing on with steadfastness in Christ unto the end (:verse[2ne.31.20]; :verse[2ne.32.3], :verse[2ne.32.8-9]). Nephi writes with declared plainness, that his teaching may be understood as direction rather than mystery (:verse[2ne.33.5-6]).

The same image appears in the ministry of Jesus. Strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it (:verse[matt.7.14]). Luke preserves the urgency: strive to enter in at the strait gate (:verse[luke.13.24]). Narrowness denotes exclusiveness of means rather than scarcity of invitation. The way is constricted because it passes only through Christ.

Isaiah had seen the same way as a highway. An highway shall be there, and it shall be called the way of holiness; the unclean shall not pass over it, and wayfaring men, though fools, shall not err therein (:verse[isa.35.8]). Error is excluded not by the traveler's cleverness but by the holiness of the road itself. Isaiah likewise connects the road with return: a remnant returns, stayed upon the Holy One in truth (:verse[isa.10.20-22]). The few who find the path correspond to the remnant who return by it.

Each element of Nephi's doctrine finds its counterpart in Isaiah's ascent. The gate is cleansing and entry: wash you, make you clean, and come now and let us reason together (:verse[isa.1.16], 18), with the psalm's entrance question of clean hands and pure heart and the lifting of gates for the King of glory (:verse[psa.24.3-9]). Baptism is purging and departure: filth washed and blood purged by burning with cloud and fire over the assembly (:verse[isa.4.4-5]), paired with going forth of Babylon with a voice of singing (:verse[isa.48.20]). The Holy Ghost is touching, pouring, and renewing: a coal from the altar taking away iniquity (:verse[isa.6.6-7]), Spirit poured upon descendants (:verse[isa.44.3]), and spirit poured from on high turning wilderness to fruitful field (:verse[isa.32.15]). Enduring is waiting, resolve, and quiet trust: they that wait shall renew their strength and mount up with wings (:verse[isa.40.31]), the Servant setting his face like a flint (:verse[isa.50.7]), and quietness and confidence as strength (:verse[isa.30.15]). The end is presence, knowledge, and feast: the earth filled with the knowledge of the Lord as waters cover the sea (:verse[isa.11.9]), the Holy One in the midst (:verse[isa.12.6]), and the mountain feast where death is swallowed, tears are wiped, and the waiting declare this is our God (:verse[isa.25.6-9]). The path that began at a gate of repentance ends at a table of presence.

Nephi's phrase preserves the identity of the path. The saved hold to the rod and press forward, feasting upon Christ as the source of endurance (:verse[2ne.31.19-20]). Jesus identifies himself as both entrance and way: the door (:verse[john.10.7-9]) and the way, the truth, and the life (:verse[john.14.6]). In this language, Christ is gate, keeper, and road at once. The non-scriptural alternative can subtly shift the emphasis, as though the path were the covenants themselves rather than the Christ to whom covenants bind. Covenants mark entrance and obligation, but they do not replace the Redeemer who makes return possible. Grace frames the whole ascent: saved by grace after all that can be done (:verse[2ne.25.23]), and denied of ungodliness only to be perfected and sanctified in Christ by grace (:verse[moro.10.32-33]). The straight and narrow path is therefore strait because it admits only the cleansed, and narrow because only Christ leads through — from gate, by the Spirit, in patient endurance, unto presence and feast.

## What those who have seen have taught

The prophets who have stood in presence do not merely report visions. They teach the mechanics of seeing, and their accounts agree across dispensations.

Moses proves degrees of seeing rather than a single event: averted face at the bush (:verse[exo.3.6]), guided pillar (:verse[exo.13.21]), bounded-mountain voice without similitude (Exodus 19; :verse[deu.4.12]), sapphire-pavement meal at feet-distance with seventy elders (:verse[exo.24.9-11]), tent friend-speech (:verse[exo.33.11]), glory-after-passing heard as the proclaimed Name (:verse[exo.33.22]–:verse[exo.34.6]), and a reflected shining that frightens others (:verse[exo.34.29-35]). The tension between face-to-face speech and no-man-sees-and-lives resolves by transfiguration: natural eyes cannot behold, but eyes quickened by glory upon the seer endure (:verse[moses.1.11]; :verse[dc67.67.11]). And Moses sought the same for Israel — to sanctify the people that they might behold — but they chose delegation, saying let not God speak with us, and hardened until they could not endure (:verse[exo.20.19]; :verse[dc84.84.23-24]). What replaced presence was law at distance, tabernacle mediation, and the veil: the tabernacle as portable Sinai trains a people for the unmediated (:verse[exo.25.8]; :verse[exo.40.34-38]).

Ezekiel adds the road glory travels. It departs in stages — cherub to threshold (:verse[ezek.9.3]), to the east gate (10:19), to the eastern mountain (11:23) — driven by catalogued abominations, with judgment beginning at the sanctuary (9:6). It returns only after shame, separation, and purging, from the east with voice like many waters (43:2-4), completing in Eden-river with healing leaves (47:1-12; :verse[rev.22.1-2]). Isaiah's summary stands over both movements: iniquities separate (:verse[isa.59.2]), and the light returns (:verse[isa.60.1-3]). Ezekiel also establishes the order of proclamation: the scroll eaten sweet as honey before the bitter commission (:verse[ezek.3.1-3]; :verse[jer.15.16]; :verse[rev.10.9-10]) — internalize before speaking, as Isaiah's coal cleanses lips before sending.

Lehi, Nephi, Jacob, and Enos establish the seeker's invariant: received word, desire, pondering, mighty prayer, being overcome and carried, seeing and hearing, praise, then public testimony under persecution (:verse[1ne.1.5-20]; :verse[1ne.11.1-6]). Nephi's tree is the summit rather than the preface — multitudes press along the strait and narrow path until they partake (:verse[1ne.8.24], 30) — and his doctrine of Christ is autobiography as doctrine (2 Nephi 31-33). Enos is the control case proving the road is not restricted to office: implanted word, hungering wrestle day and night, voice of remission, ever-widening intercession, covenant rest (:verse[enos.1.2-18]).

The brother of Jared demonstrates the veil's mechanics: sixteen stones carried one by one, plain asking for light, finger seen in fear, exceeding faith that could not be kept from beholding within the veil, then the full person with Father-and-Son doctrine and a panoramic past-to-future, sealed until due time (Ether 3-4). Moroni's key follows: rend the veil of unbelief with broken heart, and greater things unfold (:verse[ether.4.13-16]); no witness until after the trial of faith (:verse[ether.12.6]). Abinadi authenticates by shining face before a hostile court, refuses recall, and seals with fire — with Alma as the fruit who writes everything (:verse[mosiah.13.5]; :verse[mosiah.17.2-10], 20). Alma's own conversion follows the chiasm of rack to joy, turning at remembered prophecy and a cried-out plea to Jesus (:verse[alma.36.17-20]), and his great sermon makes the mighty change examinable: born of God, image in the countenance, garments white, rest entered (:verse[alma.5.14-35]; :verse[alma.12.34-37]; :verse[alma.13.11-13]).

Bountiful is the template communal theophany: voice piercing three times before ears open, the descending Man, the identical Beloved-Son formula of three dispensations, thrust-hands one by one, doctrine with a see-my-face promise, the perfection command, compassion and children, sacrament, groaning prayer with a transfigured-white multitude and ministering angels, and a sealed unspeakable portion (3 Nephi 11; :verse[3ne.12.48]; 3 Nephi 17-19; :verse[3ne.28.14]). Paul supplies the New Testament's own veil theology: the Mosaic face-veil become heart-veil removed in Christ, beholding with open face transforming into the same image (:verse[2cor.3.7-18]; :verse[2cor.4.6]), with grace-sufficient restraint on the unspeakable (:verse[2cor.12.4-9]). Joseph replays the canon in order — confusion, :verse[james.1.5], retirement, seizure by darkness, delivering pillar, two Personages, Hear Him (:verse[jsh.1.5-20]) — and Kirtland replays Sinai-to-tabernacle with veil from minds, flame-eyes, rushing-waters voice, and keys (D&C 110). Enoch gives the social end: individual visions culminating in a taken-up city of one heart and mind (:verse[moses.7.18], :verse[moses.7.21], :verse[moses.7.62-64]).

Together they teach one curriculum: separation to a high or secret place, light and glory with fear and confession, purging and transfiguration since unquickened flesh cannot endure, seeing of throne or face or wounds, voice naming with commission, the doctrine of Christ taught as the way itself, charge to testify and record, a sealed unspeakable portion, and a shining return to ministry.

## Becoming visitable

Isaiah does not teach technique for compelling visits. He teaches formation for abiding them: reasoned honesty, washing, ascent to instruction, purging at the altar, reverent hearing over human alliance, willingness to be carried, awakening and cleansing, covenant clothing, Sabbath delight, fasting that lifts burdens, contrition that hosts indwelling, and watchful blessing by night.

The end is not ecstasy but residence. A holy mountain without hurt, a highway where the unclean do not pass yet wayfarers do not err, a feast with veil destroyed, watchmen seeing eye to eye, and the Holy One in the midst. In Nephi's words, those who see these words may lift up their hearts and rejoice. In the Lord's words through Joseph Smith, the sanctified and single shall see him unveiled. The work, in every dispensation, is the same: become clean, contrite, and gathered enough to remain.

## What review found missing — seven load-bearing themes

A later review of the full project found seven themes that must be stated to keep the ascent from becoming mere effort or private experience.

Grace creates capacity, not only rewards it. Nephi holds both saved by grace after all that can be done, Moroni holds both deny ungodliness and love God wholly, then grace sufficient to be perfected and sanctified spotless, and the Doctrine and Covenants holds both justification and sanctification through grace unto sainthood (:verse[2ne.25.23]; :verse[moro.10.32-33]; :verse[dc20.20.31-34]). Surrender brings willingness; grace provides water, blood, fire, and oil.

Justice explains why holiness is required. Law demands separation, and mercy cannot rob justice except through one who subjects flesh to Spirit and intercedes (:verse[alma.42.13-24]; :verse[2ne.9.19-26]; :verse[mosiah.15.9]). Washing is therefore rescue rather than improvement. Without a Satisfier, trembling would end in consuming fire rather than indwelling.

Ordinances mark the straight and narrow path. Isaiah trains affections, but priesthood ordinances confer the power of godliness by which the face is seen (:verse[dc84.84.19-24]). The sequence corresponds across the ascent: washing toward baptism, burning toward the Holy Ghost, awakening and garments toward further endowment, tent enlargement and new name toward sealing and the narrow way's end (Isaiah 1; 4; 6; 52; 54; 62; :verse[2ne.31.17-20]; :verse[matt.7.13-14]; :verse[moses.6.60-61]).

A note on terms: this article uses "straight and narrow path" — Nephi's phrase for the way that leads to eternal life through faith, repentance, baptism, the Holy Ghost, and enduring to the end (:verse[2ne.31.17-20]) — rather than "covenant path," which does not occur as a phrase in scripture. Covenant language is kept where scripture uses it: covenant people, covenant clothing, new and everlasting covenant.

Eden frames the return. Exile eastward guarded by cherubim becomes highway return to the holy mountain, wilderness becomes Eden, and the destroyed veil becomes tree and river restored where servants see his face (Genesis 2-3; :verse[isa.51.3]; :verse[rev.22.1-5]). Washing is re-entry, garments are coverings restored, rest is Sabbath dwelling.

Zion is communal. No private perfection suffices, for all are the work of his hand. Zion is one heart with no poor, created by enlarged tents, taught children, tables become altars, and strangers seated (:verse[moses.7.18]; :verse[4ne.1.2-17]; :verse[isa.54.1-5]; :verse[isa.66.7-13]; :verse[moses.5.11]). Every fast and Sabbath is judged by whether it made one heart.

Prisoners include the dead. The Servant opens eyes and brings the bound from prison, Christ opens the same between death and resurrection, and Elijah welds ancestors to descendants (:verse[isa.49.9]; :verse[isa.61.1]; :verse[1pet.3.19-20]; :verse[1pet.4.6]; :verse[dc128.128.15-18]; 138). Ascent therefore carries names in both directions, for the living and the dead.

The end is named and opposition is expected. The sanctified may receive the Second Comforter, Christ manifesting himself to those who love and keep, and all shall stand resurrected before the bar, the contrite entering rest (:verse[john.14.16-23]; :verse[dc130.130.3]; :verse[2ne.9.14-16]; :verse[alma.12.34-37]). Between now and then, opposition proves agency and the furnace chooses, while quiet confidence remains strength (:verse[2ne.2.11]; :verse[isa.48.10]; :verse[isa.30.15]). Added years, prison, and Gethsemane belong to the same curriculum: presence now is earnest, fullness later.

## Beyond the canon: what neighboring texts add

The traditions surrounding ancient Israel preserved ascent imagery the canon assumes but rarely explains. Read with a three-bucket rule — *confirm* (same doctrine, independent voice), *illuminate* (compatible color), *sectarian* (do not import) — these texts sharpen rather than replace the scriptural account.

The closest parallels are three. The Ascension of Isaiah (chs. 6-11) expands Isaiah 6 into a guided seven-heaven ascent with graded glory, reserved robes, and a descending Redeemer — devotional gold in a composite document with second-century Christian edges. The Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice at Qumran offer thirteen Sabbaths of angelic priesthoods and throne-chariot praise, notably found at Masada as well as Qumran, suggesting wider circulation: earliest Jewish mysticism was congregational, earthly choir singing with angels, a direct precedent for the united prayer of 3 Nephi 19 and the D&C 109 dedication. The Odes of Solomon give the strongest presence-texts outside canon — paradise given now, rest from above, sun-face dispelling darkness, the soul "carried like a child by its mother" — best read alongside 3 Nephi 17's children and Psalm 131, with the caution that some odes lack an explicit atonement framework.

The temple and purity systems cut both ways. The Community Rule (1QS) states pre-Christian temple-ecclesiology plainly — the united community as "temple... Holy of Holies" with examination, washing, and annual covenant entry, a Psalms 24 gate liturgy in rule form — but restricts it by predestination, permanent exclusion of the blemished, and hatred of outsiders. The Hodayot supply transformation language nearest Alma 5 and :verse[moro.10.32-33] anywhere outside canon, yet election stays insider-only. The Melchizedek scroll (11Q13) proves first-century Jews awaited a heavenly priest-judge bringing Jubilee liberty on Atonement Day, quoting :verse[isa.52.7] and 61:1-2 — Alma 13 and Hebrews 7 land in a live expectation. The pattern across Qumran: same architecture of gate, washing, garment, and song — but courts made smaller by exclusion where prophecy makes them larger by invitation (:verse[2ne.26.33]).

Wisdom deserves its own hearing, since seeking wisdom is the appointed start of seeing. Proverbs personifies Wisdom crying at the gates with two banquets set — Wisdom's feast of life against Folly's stolen waters leading to death — and Job 28 confesses wisdom hidden from all living, disclosed by God to the reverent who depart from evil. Sirach 24 gives Wisdom's autobiography: come forth from the Most High's mouth, throned in cloud, searching the earth for rest, commanded to dwell in Jacob and minister in the holy tabernacle — universal Wisdom lodging in particular Zion, Torah, and temple. Wisdom of Solomon 7-9 is the fullest meditation: twenty-one attributes of a holy spirit, "breath of the power of God" and "brightness of everlasting light," entering holy souls to make "friends of God, and prophets," climaxing in throne-prayer for wisdom and the sending of the Holy Spirit from above. The counterpoint stands in 1 Enoch 42, where Wisdom finds no dwelling among men and returns to heaven — refused Wisdom leaves a vacuum unrighteousness fills, as :verse[john.1.11] later confirms.

The New Testament reads Lady Wisdom as heir in Christ: wisdom justified of her children (:verse[matt.11.19]), Christ the power and wisdom of God (:verse[1cor.1.24]), image of the invisible God and firstborn in whom all things cohere (:verse[col.1.15-20], echoing Proverbs 8 and Wisdom 7), the Word tenting among us (:verse[john.1.14], echoing Sirach 24:8), wisdom from above pure and peaceable for all who ask (:verse[james.1.5]; :verse[james.3.17]). Latter-day scripture supplies the medium: the Light of Christ proceeding from God's presence to fill immensity, enlightening every person (:verse[dc88.88.6-13]; :verse[john.1.9]; :verse[moro.7.16]), growing by obedience into intelligence until the single eye, filled with light, comprehends and at last sees face to face (:verse[dc88.88.67-68]; :verse[dc93.93.1]; :verse[matt.5.8]). Some LDS writers, following Margaret Barker's First-Temple reconstruction, hear Wisdom echoes in Lehi's and Nephi's throne visions and tree imagery — useful as comparative illumination, with a firm boundary: the Church affirms a real, divine Heavenly Mother while directing against speculative identification with Wisdom figures, and Barker's reconstruction remains minority scholarship. Use it as lens, not doctrine.

The discernment holds for the whole shelf: traditions that take presence seriously converge on gate, washing, garment, song, and veil, and diverge on whether the door narrows by exclusion or widens by invitation. The canon at its best does both — narrow as Christ, wide as all are alike unto God.
