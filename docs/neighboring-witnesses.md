# Neighboring Witnesses: Ascent Texts Surrounding the Canon

## Abstract

This paper surveys ancient ascent-adjacent traditions — pseudepigraphal ascents, Qumran liturgies, Wisdom books, and Philo — and argues they sharpen rather than replace the scriptural account of ascent to presence. Its method is a three-bucket rule stated up front: *confirm* (same doctrine, independent voice), *illuminate* (compatible color), *sectarian* (do not import). Its finding is that traditions taking presence seriously converge on gate, washing, garment, song, and veil, and diverge on whether the door narrows by exclusion or widens by invitation.

The canon, the paper concludes, at its best does both: narrow as Christ, wide as all are alike unto God.

## Introduction

The problem is that the canon assumes ascent imagery it rarely explains: robes, choirs, gates, and veils appear without stage directions. The question is whether the literature surrounding ancient Israel can supply the missing staging without becoming a second canon. Why it matters is devotional and scholarly at once: these texts preserve the idiom the prophets spoke in. This paper therefore proceeds as a study appendix, not doctrine: first the closest ascent parallels, then temple and purity systems, then Wisdom, then the New Testament and latter-day synthesis, then Philo — applying the three-bucket discernment throughout.

## Method: the three-bucket rule

The rule governs every text below.

*Confirm* means the text independently voices the same doctrine the canon teaches.

*Illuminate* means it supplies compatible color — vocabulary, setting, or practice — without binding interpretation.

*Sectarian* means its distinctive claim (predestination, exclusion, speculative cosmology) must not be imported.

They illuminate; they do not bind.

## The closest parallels: guided ascent, angelic choir, present paradise

Three texts stand nearest the scriptural ascent pattern, and each needs an introduction.

The *Ascension of Isaiah* (chapters 6–11) is an early Christian-era composite that expands Isaiah 6 into a guided seven-heaven ascent with graded glory, reserved robes, and a descending Redeemer. What it argues, in effect, is that Isaiah's throne vision opens onto layered heavens through which the seer is led. Why it matters here is the wardrobe detail: glory graded by heaven and robes held in reserve gives concrete idiom to the scriptural pairing of washing and robing. Bucket: devotional gold in places, *illuminate* — but the document is composite with second-century Christian edges, so nothing in it binds.

The *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice* are thirteen Sabbath liturgies from Qumran, found notably at Masada as well as Qumran. Who they are: the earliest Jewish mystical liturgy we possess, structured around angelic priesthoods and throne-chariot praise. What they argue by form is that mysticism was congregational — an earthly choir singing with angels. Why that matters here is the precedent: wider circulation (Masada plus Qumran) suggests this was not a sectarian peculiarity, and it supplies a direct precedent for the united prayer of 3 Nephi 19 and the D&C 109 dedication. Bucket: *illuminate* moving toward *confirm* on the single point that ascent praise is sung together.

The *Odes of Solomon* are early Christian hymns giving the strongest presence-texts outside canon: paradise given now, rest from above, sun-face dispelling darkness, the soul "carried like a child by its mother." What the quoted lines say is that rest and paradise are present tense, not only future; what that means is that the Odist treats presence as tasted before death; why it supports the paper is the pairing it invites — best read alongside 3 Nephi 17's children and Psalm 131, where lowliness and childlikeness condition presence. Bucket: *illuminate*, with the stated caution that some odes lack an explicit atonement framework and therefore cannot carry doctrinal weight alone.

## Temple and purity systems: same architecture, smaller courts

The Qumran rule and hymn books cut both ways, and each author-group needs naming.

The movement here is from shared architecture to narrowed courts: what Qumran shares with the ascent pattern, and where it closes what prophecy opens.

The *Community Rule* (1QS) is the sect's ordering document: a pre-Christian temple-ecclesiology stating plainly that the united community is itself "temple … Holy of Holies," entered by examination, washing, and annual covenant entry — a Psalms 24 gate liturgy in rule form. What it says is that holiness is embodied in a prepared community; what it means is that gate, washing, and covenant predate any single restoration idiom; why it matters is the architecture it shares with the ascent pattern. But its courts are smaller: predestination, permanent exclusion of the blemished, and hatred of outsiders restrict what prophecy widens. Bucket: *illuminate* on form, *sectarian* on exclusion.

The *Hodayot* (Thanksgiving Hymns) are the sect's first-person transformation psalms. What they supply is transformation language nearest Alma 5 and :verse[moro.10.32-33] anywhere outside canon — creature-to-son idiom, cleansing and lifting. What that means is that the vocabulary of being changed resembles the Book of Mormon's conversion vocabulary. Yet election stays insider-only. Bucket: same split — *illuminate* on language, *sectarian* on boundary.

The *Melchizedek scroll* (11Q13) is a first-century eschatological midrash — an interpretive retelling of scripture — proclaiming a heavenly priest-judge bringing Jubilee liberty on the Day of Atonement, quoting :verse[isa.52.7] and Isaiah 61:1-2. Who he is in the scroll: Melchizedek as heavenly deliverer. What the scroll argues is that at least some in this circle awaited exactly such a figure. Why it matters is context: Alma 13 and Hebrews 7 land in a live expectation rather than a vacuum. Bucket: *confirm* on expectation, *illuminate* on detail. The pattern across Qumran is therefore the paper's hinge sentence: same architecture of gate, washing, garment, and song, but courts made smaller by exclusion where prophecy makes them larger by invitation (:verse[2ne.26.33]).

## Wisdom: the appointed start of seeing

Wisdom deserves its own hearing, since seeking wisdom is the appointed start of seeing.

Four witnesses plus a counterpoint follow, moving from invitation (Proverbs) through gift (Job), residence (Sirach), and fullness (Wisdom of Solomon) to refusal (1 Enoch).

Proverbs personifies Wisdom crying at the gates, setting two banquets against each other: Wisdom's feast of life against Folly's stolen waters leading to death. Who Wisdom is here: a female figure voicing God's invitation in public. What the passage argues is that the gate is a choice between feasts. Job 28 confesses wisdom hidden from all living, disclosed by God to the reverent who depart from evil — wisdom as gift, not find. Sirach 24, a second-century B.C. wisdom book, gives Wisdom's autobiography: come forth from the Most High's mouth, throned in cloud, searching the earth for rest, commanded to dwell in Jacob and minister in the holy tabernacle. What that says is universal Wisdom lodging in particular Zion, Torah, and temple; what it means for this paper is that Wisdom seeks residence, culminating in tabernacle ministry. Wisdom of Solomon 7–9, a later Alexandrian meditation, is the fullest of all: twenty-one attributes of a holy spirit, "breath of the power of God" and "brightness of everlasting light," entering holy souls to make "friends of God, and prophets," climaxing in throne-prayer for wisdom and the sending of the Holy Spirit from above. Unpacked: wisdom is personal, holy, and sent; it makes prophets by entering them; prayer is its means. The counterpoint stands in 1 Enoch 42, where Wisdom finds no dwelling among men and returns to heaven. What that says is refusal has consequences; what it means is that rejected Wisdom leaves a vacuum unrighteousness fills, as :verse[john.1.11] later confirms ("his own received him not").

The New Testament reads Lady Wisdom as heir in Christ: wisdom justified of her children (:verse[matt.11.19]) — what is said is that deeds vindicate the teaching; Christ the power and wisdom of God (:verse[1cor.1.24]); image of the invisible God and firstborn in whom all things cohere (:verse[col.1.15-20], echoing Proverbs 8 and Wisdom 7) — what is said is cosmic coherence in a person; the Word tenting among us (:verse[john.1.14], echoing Sirach 24:8) — tabernacle idiom become incarnation; wisdom from above pure and peaceable for all who ask (:verse[james.1.5]; :verse[james.3.17]).

Latter-day scripture supplies the medium: the Light of Christ proceeding from God's presence to fill immensity, enlightening every person (:verse[dc88.88.6-13]; :verse[john.1.9]; :verse[moro.7.16]), growing by obedience into intelligence until the single eye, filled with light, comprehends and at last sees face to face (:verse[dc88.88.67-68]; :verse[dc93.93.1]; :verse[matt.5.8]). Some LDS writers, following Margaret Barker's First-Temple reconstruction — Barker being the Methodist scholar who argues Josiah's reform suppressed temple Wisdom imagery — hear Wisdom echoes in Lehi's and Nephi's throne visions and tree imagery. That is useful as comparative illumination, with a firm boundary the paper keeps: the Church affirms a real, divine Heavenly Mother while directing against speculative identification with Wisdom figures, and Barker's reconstruction remains minority scholarship. Use it as lens, not doctrine.

## Philo of Alexandria: the Logos as priest and divider

Philo shows a learned Jew of Paul's century reaching for mediation by one who belongs to both sides. Survey-level only (treatise names, no section citations): lens, not doctrine. Philo of Alexandria, the first-century Jewish philosopher of the Diaspora, reasons about a mediator between the utterly transcendent God and creation: the Logos (the divine Word or Reason as mediator). In *On the Creation* the Logos is God's instrument and pattern in making the world — derivative divinity as craftsman rather than fellow Creator (cf. the explicit instrument formula at *De Cherubim* 127; the *Opif.* locus proper is §§24–25, the Logos as archetypal plan). What that says is mediation by design; what it means in Philo's Middle Platonist system is emanation grades — reality thinning out in descending levels — not incarnation. In the dream and flight treatises (*On Dreams*, *On Flight*) the Logos appears as heavenly high priest interceding above (cf. *De Fuga* §§108–112, Logos as *archiereus*, literally "high priest"), and in *Who Is the Heir* as the great divider and cutter, separating opposites the way the word divides light from darkness (the *tomeus*, "cutter," exegesis; mediator passages at §§205–206). The architecture will feel familiar from Hebrews and John: priesthood above, mediation by one who belongs to both sides, division by utterance. Philo's system stays Middle Platonist: emanation grades, allegorized ancestors, a God too pure to touch matter, and the Church has never canonized a line of him. Use him as comparative illumination on how a learned Jew of Paul's century thought about mediation, with the firm boundary the rest of this appendix keeps: the canon at its best does both — narrow as Christ, wide as all are alike unto God.

## Limits and objections

Two limits apply to the whole shelf. First, dating and composite authorship (Ascension of Isaiah, Odes, Qumran scrolls) mean no single text can bear the weight of a doctrine; the paper therefore claims convergence of idiom, not dependence. Second, the Wisdom-to-Christ and Barker lines risk over-reading; the paper marks them explicitly as lens, and any extension to Heavenly Mother theology would need separate warrant from current Church statements and handbook direction before this appendix is ever taught. On Barker specifically, the skeptics' case deserves one paragraph: mainstream critics judge the Josianic-suppression thesis under-evidenced — a lost First-Temple Wisdom cult reconstructed largely from texts that postdate the alleged suppression — so Barker functions here as comparative illumination that suggests where to look, never as proof of what was there. If the suppression thesis fails, the Wisdom parallels remain interesting as idiom and lose their force as restoration evidence; the paper's buckets already price that in.

## Conclusion

The discernment holds for the whole shelf: traditions that take presence seriously converge on gate, washing, garment, song, and veil, and diverge on whether the door narrows by exclusion or widens by invitation. The canon at its best does both: narrow as Christ, wide as all are alike unto God.
